## CASE seed-growth-d2c — growth · Beauty & personal care (D2C) · standard, ~25 min
**Opening (read this aloud to start):** Our client is Vanaspa, a digital-first Ayurvedic skincare brand. It did about ₹120 crore of revenue last year and its investors want it to reach about ₹360 crore within three years, ahead of a possible IPO. The founders have asked us how they should get there.
**Objective (share if asked):** Identify the growth levers and a plan to reach ~₹360 crore revenue in three years without destroying unit economics.
**Background (share only the part asked about):** Founded 2018. Hero products: a kumkumadi face oil and an ubtan face wash. Sells through its own website, marketplaces (Amazon, Nykaa) and a small offline pilot. Contribution-margin positive but EBITDA negative due to marketing spend.
**Clarification answers (give only the one asked about):**
- profitability constraint: Investors want growth, but they will not fund a plan in which marketing spend grows faster than revenue. The company should reach EBITDA break-even by year three.
- product portfolio: About 40 SKUs; the top 5 hero products are ~65% of revenue. Face care is the core category.
- geography: India only. About 70% of revenue comes from the top 8 metros.
**Data — release ONLY when its condition is met:**
- Revenue by channel, last year: Own website ₹50 cr, marketplaces ₹52 cr, offline pilot ₹18 cr (about 200 modern-trade and pharmacy doors). _(release when: Candidate asks for revenue split by channel.)_
- Customer metrics: About 12 lakh active customers; average order value ₹700; ~1.43 orders per customer per year. 12-month repeat rate is 22%; category leaders are around 40%. _(release when: Candidate breaks revenue into customers × frequency × order value, or asks about retention/repeat.)_
- Digital acquisition cost trend: Blended customer acquisition cost (CAC) on digital rose from ₹450 two years ago to ₹900 now. First-order contribution margin is about ₹280. _(release when: Candidate asks about marketing efficiency, CAC, or scaling digital acquisition.)_
- Offline opportunity: The offline pilot doors average about ₹9 lakh revenue per door per year at ~25% contribution after trade margins, with no customer acquisition cost. There are roughly 8,000 relevant modern-trade and premium-pharmacy doors in the top 30 cities. New doors typically do about two-thirds of pilot productivity. _(release when: Candidate asks about offline/retail expansion or the offline pilot.)_
**Exhibit "Channel economics, last year"** _(release when: Candidate asks to compare channels or asks where growth has come from.)_
| Channel | Revenue (₹ cr) | Contribution margin after marketing | 12-month repeat rate | Growth vs prior year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Own website | 50 | 4% | 22% | +8% |
| Marketplaces | 52 | 9% | 18% | +15% |
| Offline pilot | 18 | 25% | n/a | +80% |
Hidden takeaways: The website is the least profitable after marketing and slowest-growing: digital acquisition is saturating. Offline has the best contribution and fastest growth, but from a small base. Repeat rates are low, so retention is a lever.
**HIDDEN — never reveal during the case:**
- Key insights: Digital acquisition has hit diminishing returns (CAC doubled; website margin after marketing is only 4%), so 'more of the same' cannot deliver 3x under the constraint. Offline modern trade and pharmacies are the largest capital-efficient lever (~₹150 cr). Retention (repeat 22% vs 40% benchmark) is a cheap lever worth ~₹40-50 cr.
- Calculations: What would it take to add ₹240 cr purely through digital acquisition? → Revenue per active customer ≈ ₹700 × 1.43 ≈ ₹1,000/yr → need ~24 lakh more active customers. At CAC ₹900 that is ~₹216 cr of acquisition spend before churn (more, given 22% repeat), which is more than current revenue. Not viable under the marketing constraint. | Size the offline lever. → ~2,500 doors × ₹6 lakh (two-thirds of ₹9 lakh) ≈ ₹150 cr in three years. | Size the retention lever. → Raising orders per customer from 1.43 to ~1.8 (repeat 22% → ~35%) on a base of ~15-18 lakh customers at ₹700 AOV adds roughly ₹40-50 cr.
- Reference solution: Clarify the constraint (no marketing-led growth). Break revenue into customers × frequency × AOV by channel. The channel exhibit shows digital is saturating and offline has the best economics. Sizing: offline ~₹150 cr (2,500 doors × ₹6 lakh), retention ~₹40-50 cr, marketplace growth and selective digital ~₹30-40 cr, and product extensions (e.g. body care from hero ingredients) ~₹20-30 cr. That gets to ~₹360 cr. Sequence: retention quick wins in year 1, offline scale-up years 1-3, adjacencies year 2+.
- Other valid approaches: An Ansoff-style view (penetration/product/market/diversification) is acceptable if the candidate prioritises with the economics instead of listing it. Inorganic growth (acquiring a complementary brand) is a valid option to discuss, especially given IPO timing, but should be tested against capital availability.
- Recommendation: Pursue a capital-efficient growth plan: (1) scale offline to ~2,500 modern-trade/pharmacy doors (~₹150 cr), with a distributor model and planograms for hero SKUs; (2) fix retention with regimen-based bundles, subscriptions and CRM (~₹40-50 cr); (3) keep digital acquisition at efficient CAC only; (4) extend hero ingredients into adjacent categories (~₹20-30 cr). Risks: offline working capital and returns, channel price conflict with marketplaces, and brand dilution. Next steps: pilot distributor model in 3 cities, and test retention levers on existing cohorts.
- Common pitfalls: Listing growth options without sizing them. Ignoring the unit-economics constraint. Forgetting to check that the levers add up to the target.

## CASE seed-entry-qcomm — market entry · E-commerce (quick commerce) · standard, ~25 min
**Opening (read this aloud to start):** Our client is ZipKart, a quick-commerce company delivering groceries in 10-15 minutes in 8 metro cities. It is profitable at the dark-store level in the metros. The board wants to enter 20 Tier-2 cities such as Indore, Nagpur, Coimbatore and Lucknow next year. Should they, and if so, how?
**Objective (share if asked):** Decide whether and how to enter Tier-2 cities profitably. The board wants each dark store to break even within 12 months of launch.
**Background (share only the part asked about):** ~600 dark stores in metros. Well funded. A competitor has announced Tier-2 plans. ZipKart ran a 3-month pilot with 4 dark stores in Indore.
**Clarification answers (give only the one asked about):**
- success criteria: Each dark store should reach break-even at the contribution-after-store-costs level within 12 months.
- business model: ZipKart earns a margin of about 18% on what it sells, plus a small delivery fee. Costs are riders, packaging and payment per order, plus the fixed costs of running each dark store.
- competition: One competitor has announced Tier-2 plans but has launched in only 2 cities so far. Local kiranas already offer phone ordering and home delivery in many neighbourhoods.
**Data — release ONLY when its condition is met:**
- Market demand signals: Target cities have 10-40 lakh people each. App downloads from these cities are already ~6% of the national base. Urban household spend on groceries is ~70% of metro levels. _(release when: Candidate asks about market size, demand or customer interest.)_
- Dark-store fixed costs: Metro dark store: ~₹8 lakh per month (rent ₹2.5 lakh, staff ₹4 lakh, other ₹1.5 lakh). Tier-2 dark store: ~₹5.5 lakh per month. _(release when: Candidate asks about store-level costs, setup or break-even.)_
- Indore pilot demand: The Indore pilot stores are at ~220 orders/day after 3 months and the trend suggests ~350 orders/day at maturity. _(release when: Candidate asks about expected order volumes or pilot results.)_
- Levers tested in pilot: Adding electronics accessories, pharmacy and gifting raised AOV in one pilot store by about 15%. A 25-30 minute promise (instead of 10-15) lets one store serve roughly 1.6x the catchment area. _(release when: Candidate asks how to improve unit economics, AOV, or density.)_
**Exhibit "Per-order economics: metro vs Indore pilot (₹ per order)"** _(release when: Candidate asks about unit economics, per-order profitability or contribution.)_
| Item | Metro | Indore pilot |
|---|---|---|
| Average order value | 550 | 380 |
| Margin at 18% | 99 | 68 |
| Delivery fee | 15 | 10 |
| Rider cost | -45 | -35 |
| Packaging & payment | -14 | -12 |
| Contribution per order | 55 | 31 |
Hidden takeaways: Tier-2 contribution per order is ~₹31 vs ₹55 in metros, mainly because AOV is ~30% lower. Lower rider cost only partly offsets the lower AOV.
**HIDDEN — never reveal during the case:**
- Key insights: Demand exists; the issue is unit economics. Tier-2 contribution per order is ~₹31 vs ₹55 in metros, driven by AOV. Break-even needs ~585 orders/day but mature stores reach ~350, so a straight copy of the metro model fails the board's criterion. Entry is viable only with a modified model: larger catchment (longer promise), higher-AOV assortment, and fewer, bigger cities first.
- Calculations: Break-even orders per day for a Tier-2 dark store → Fixed cost ₹5.5 lakh/month ≈ ₹18,300/day. Contribution ₹31/order → ~590 orders/day (≈583 with ₹31.4). Metro: ₹8 lakh/month ≈ ₹26,700/day ÷ ₹55 ≈ 485 orders/day. | Gap at expected maturity → Expected 350 orders/day vs break-even ~585 → about 60% of break-even. Loss ≈ (585-350) × ₹31 ≈ ₹7,300/day ≈ ₹2.2 lakh per store per month.
- Reference solution: Market entry reframed as a unit-economics problem. Check market attractiveness (it exists), then per-order and store economics: contribution ₹31/order × expected 350 orders/day doesn't cover ₹18,300/day of fixed costs (break-even ~585). Levers: AOV +15% (assortment) → contribution ~₹42 (margin 0.18 × 437 ≈ 79 + 10 - 35 - 12 ≈ ₹42); a 1.6x catchment raises orders to ~560 → ~₹23,500/day contribution vs ₹18,300 fixed cost, so the store breaks even. Recommend a staged entry: 4-5 largest Tier-2 cities with the modified model, then expand based on store-level break-even.
- Other valid approaches: Asset-light entry: partner with local kiranas or supermarkets as fulfilment points instead of dark stores. Acquisition of a regional grocery delivery player, if one exists, as a way to buy density.
- Recommendation: Don't roll out the metro model to 20 cities. Enter the 4-5 largest Tier-2 cities with a modified model: a 25-30 minute promise to widen catchments, and an assortment that adds higher-AOV categories. The pilot data suggest this reaches break-even (~₹42 × ~560 orders ≈ ₹23.5k/day vs ₹18.3k fixed). Gate further expansion on store-level break-even within 12 months. Risks: the competitor moving first, customer acceptance of a slower promise, and kirana responses. Next steps: run the modified model in 2 Indore stores for 8 weeks.
- Common pitfalls: Treating it as a pure market attractiveness exercise. Saying 'enter because the competitor is entering'. Not converting monthly fixed cost to daily before dividing.

## CASE seed-growth-kabaddi-short — growth · Sports · short, ~15 min
**Opening (read this aloud to start):** Our client owns a professional kabaddi franchise based in Jaipur. Revenue is about ₹60 crore a year and the owner wants to grow it by 50% in two years. How would you think about it?
**Objective (share if asked):** Find the most promising ways to grow franchise revenue by ~₹30 crore in two years.
**Background (share only the part asked about):** League revenues are shared centrally (media rights), plus franchise-level sponsorship, ticketing and merchandise. Season lasts ~3 months; home games are ~11 matches.
**Clarification answers (give only the one asked about):**
- revenue breakdown: Central media-rights share ₹35 cr (fixed by the league), sponsorships ₹18 cr, ticketing ₹4 cr, merchandise & other ₹3 cr.
- stadium / capacity: Home arena seats ~4,500; average occupancy ~70%; average ticket ~₹800.
- fan base: ~2.5 million social followers, mostly Rajasthan and Haryana; the team finished in the top 4 last season.
**Data — release ONLY when its condition is met:**
- Sponsorship benchmark: Top franchises in the league earn ₹30-35 cr in sponsorship; they sell 12-15 sponsor slots (jersey front, back, sleeves, arena, digital), while the client sells 7. _(release when: Candidate asks about sponsorship, benchmarks or inventory.)_
- Off-season engagement: The franchise runs no off-season events. Two other franchises run youth academies and a state-level league that generate ₹4-6 cr each in fees and sponsorship. _(release when: Candidate asks about off-season/year-round revenue or community/academy ideas.)_
**HIDDEN — never reveal during the case:**
- Key insights: ₹35 cr of ₹60 cr is fixed central revenue, so the 50% growth must come from ~₹25 cr of controllable revenue, i.e. more than doubling it. Ticketing is capacity-capped (a few crore at most); sponsorship inventory (7 vs 12-15 slots) is the main lever (~₹12-15 cr). Year-round properties (academy, state league) are the second lever (~₹5 cr).
- Calculations: Ticketing upside → 11 games × 4,500 × 70% × ₹800 ≈ ₹2.8 cr (reported ₹4 cr includes premium seats/boxes). Even at 100% occupancy and +25% price, ticketing adds only ~₹2-3 cr.
- Reference solution: Separate fixed and controllable revenue. Ticketing is capacity-constrained. Sponsorship is under-monetised versus top franchises: expanding inventory (digital, arena, sleeves) and packaging it with content could add ₹12-15 cr. Launch year-round properties (academy, state league, content) for ~₹5 cr, and build merchandise with the fan base for ~₹2-4 cr. Reaching +₹30 cr is stretched; ~₹20-25 cr is realistic in two years.
- Other valid approaches: A fan-lifecycle approach (acquire, engage, monetise) is valid if it gets to the sponsorship lever. Pushing back on the target (+50% is unrealistic given the fixed central share) is good business judgment.
- Recommendation: Focus on sponsorship inventory expansion (+₹12-15 cr) and year-round properties (+~₹5 cr), plus merchandise; tell the owner that +₹20-25 cr is realistic in two years and +₹30 cr needs a stronger on-field brand or a second arena market. Risks: sponsor demand depends on team performance and viewership.
- Common pitfalls: Spending time on ticket pricing, which is capped. Not separating fixed central revenue.

## CASE seed-gs-chai-station — guesstimate · Food & beverage / Railways · short, ~12 min
**Opening (read this aloud to start):** How many cups of tea are sold in a day at Bengaluru City railway station (Majestic)?
**Objective (share if asked):** Estimate daily cups of tea sold at the station, with a clear, defensible logic.
**Background (share only the part asked about):** A chai-vending startup is considering bidding for stall space at the station.
**Clarification answers (give only the one asked about):**
- scope: All tea sold within the station premises (platforms, concourse, stalls, and vendors on the platforms). Excludes shops outside the station.
- purpose: A chai startup wants to know if a stall there would be worth bidding for.
- tea type: Any hot tea, including cutting chai, regular chai and packaged tea sold in cups.
**Data — release ONLY when its condition is met:**
- Footfall hint (only if the candidate asks for data): We don't have exact figures. Assume what you think is reasonable, and tell me why. _(release when: Candidate asks for footfall or passenger numbers.)_
**HIDDEN — never reveal during the case:**
- Key insights: Segment users (long-distance passengers with waiting time vs short-hop commuters vs staff and vendors). Supply-side cross-check (stalls × throughput) is the natural sanity check.
- Reference solution: Demand: ~2 lakh people/day pass through (passengers ~1.5 lakh + companions/staff ~0.5 lakh). Long-distance passengers (~40%) have waiting time: ~35% buy, 1.2 cups each → ~25k cups. Short-hop commuters (~45%): ~10% buy → ~7k. Companions/staff/porters (~15%): ~40% buy, 1.5 cups → ~18k. Total ≈ 50k cups/day. Supply check: ~25 stalls + ~40 platform vendors; stalls ~120 cups/hour at peak for 6 hours and ~40/hour for 12 hours → ~1,200 cups/stall/day → 30k; vendors ~400/day each → 16k; ≈ 46k. Reasonable range: 30-70k cups/day.
- Other valid approaches: Supply-side-only estimate with explicit stall throughput. Train-based: number of trains × passengers per train × purchase rate.
- Recommendation: About 45-55k cups a day. For the startup: at ~₹15/cup that's a ₹7-8 lakh/day market at the station, so a single stall with ~3-5% share would sell ~1,500-2,500 cups a day, which helps evaluate the stall bid.
- Common pitfalls: No segmentation (applying one purchase rate to everyone). No sanity check. Ignoring platform vendors.
- Reasonable range: 30,000 – 70,000 cups/day

- geography: Delhi NCR: Delhi, Gurugram, Noida, Ghaziabad and Faridabad.
- time frame: Annual, in rupees, for the current year.
**Data — release ONLY when its condition is met:**
**HIDDEN — never reveal during the case:**

## CASE seed-gs-ev-chargers-blr — guesstimate · Automotive / Energy · standard, ~18 min
**Opening (read this aloud to start):** How many public EV charging points will Bengaluru need by 2030?
**Objective (share if asked):** Estimate the number of public charging points (not home chargers) needed in 2030.
**Background (share only the part asked about):** A charge-point operator is planning its city roll-out.
**Clarification answers (give only the one asked about):**
- scope: Public charging points (a point = one connector that can charge one vehicle at a time), for cars, two-wheelers, three-wheelers and cabs. Home and captive fleet depots are excluded.
- charger types: Assume a mix of fast DC chargers for cars/cabs and slower AC/swap points for two-wheelers. You can simplify.
**Data — release ONLY when its condition is met:**
**HIDDEN — never reveal during the case:**
- Key insights: Segmenting by vehicle type matters: cabs and three-wheelers depend on public charging far more than private cars. Charger utilisation (not 24x7 at 100%) drives the answer.
- Reference solution: 2030 parc: two-wheelers ~90 lakh, cars ~30 lakh, cabs ~2 lakh, 3W ~2 lakh. EV share: 2W 30% (27 lakh), cars 15% (4.5 lakh), cabs 60% (1.2 lakh), 3W 70% (1.4 lakh). Public dependence: 2W 15% → 4 lakh, cars 25% → 1.1 lakh, cabs 80% → ~1 lakh, 3W 60% → 0.85 lakh. Daily energy: 2W 2 kWh, cars 8, cabs 30, 3W 8. Daily energy totals: 2W 4 lakh × 2 = 0.8 GWh; cars 1.1 lakh × 8 = 0.9 GWh; cabs 1 lakh × 30 = 3.0 GWh; 3W 0.85 lakh × 8 = 0.7 GWh → ~5.4 GWh/day. Per point: fast DC 30 kW × 8 effective hours ≈ 240 kWh/day; AC 7 kW × 8 h ≈ 56. Cars/cabs/3W on DC: 4.6 GWh ÷ 240 ≈ 19k; 2W on AC: 0.8 GWh ÷ 56 ≈ 14k. Total ≈ 30-35k points.
- Other valid approaches: Benchmark ratio method (e.g., ~1 public point per 10-15 EVs globally, adjusted for home-charging availability). Location-based: charging hubs per km² in dense zones plus highway/corridor points.
- Recommendation: Roughly 30-35k public charging points, dominated by fast chargers for cabs and commercial vehicles. The operator should prioritise cab/3W-heavy hubs over residential car charging.
- Common pitfalls: No vehicle segmentation. Assuming 100% utilisation. Mixing up chargers and charging stations.
- Reasonable range: 15,000 – 60,000 points

## CASE seed-mna-cdmo — mna · Pharmaceuticals (CDMO / APIs) · standard, ~30 min
**Opening (read this aloud to start):** Our client is a mid-sized Indian contract development and manufacturing organisation (a CDMO) with about ₹2,400 crore in revenue. It has been offered the chance to acquire Vizag-based Andhra Fine Chem, an active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) manufacturer, for about ₹1,800 crore. The CEO wants to know whether they should do the deal.
**Objective (share if asked):** Recommend whether to acquire the target, and on what terms.
**Background (share only the part asked about):** The client makes formulations and advanced intermediates for global pharma companies under contract. About 30% of its key starting materials and intermediates are imported from China. The target is family-owned; the family wants to exit within 6 months.
**Clarification answers (give only the one asked about):**
- rationale / why now: Two reasons: supply security (a 2023 China supply disruption stopped two of the client's product lines for 7 weeks), and customers increasingly want 'China+1' suppliers for APIs.
- financing: The client can fund the deal with cash and debt; net debt/EBITDA would rise to about 2.5x, which the board accepts.
- deadline / process: There is one other interested bidder, a PE fund. Binding bids are due in 8 weeks.
**Data — release ONLY when its condition is met:**
- Valuation benchmark: Listed Indian API peers trade at 13-15x EV/EBITDA; recent private API deals were done at 11-13x. _(release when: Candidate asks how the price compares, or about multiples/valuation.)_
- Synergy estimates (management): Procurement/backward-integration savings ~₹40 cr a year from year 1; cross-selling APIs to the client's customers could add ~₹25 cr EBITDA by year 3. One-off integration cost ~₹60 cr. _(release when: Candidate asks about synergies or what the combined entity gains.)_
- Regulatory diligence: The target's main plant received a USFDA Form 483 with 6 observations in 2024. The response has been filed, but a re-inspection is pending. Risks: cannibalisation of higher-margin items, competitor price response, and the need to protect average spend.

## CASE seed-prof-dairy — operations · Dairy / Agri-processing · standard, ~25 min
**Opening (read this aloud to start):** Our client is a state dairy cooperative in Karnataka. It collects about 10 lakh litres of milk a day from around 2,000 village societies and sells packaged milk and dairy products. Its net margin has fallen from about 3% to under 1% over two years, even though volumes have grown slightly. The managing director wants to understand why and what to do.
**Objective (share if asked):** Explain the margin decline and recommend actions to restore margins to ~3% without cutting farmer procurement prices.
**Background (share only the part asked about):** Farmer-owned cooperative; mandate to pay farmers fair procurement prices. Milk flows from village societies → bulk milk chillers (BMCs) at collection centres or directly to plants within ~30 km → processing plants → distributors. About 70% of sales is liquid milk; the rest is curd, ghee and skimmed milk powder (SMP).
**Clarification answers (give only the one asked about):**
- business model / products: About 70% of revenue is liquid milk; the rest is curd, ghee and skimmed milk powder. Milk that can't be sold as liquid milk is converted into lower-value products like SMP and ghee.
- constraints: As a cooperative, cutting the procurement price paid to farmers is not an option. Consumer price increases need state approval and are politically sensitive.
- supply chain: Village societies pour milk into bulk milk chillers (BMCs) at collection centres, which cool it to 4°C before tankers take it to plants. Societies close to plants deliver directly.
- competition: Private dairies are growing in cities, but the client's market share in packaged milk is stable.
**Data — release ONLY when its condition is met:**
- Economics per litre (₹): FY23 → FY25: sales realisation 52.0 → 54.9; procurement price paid to farmers 38.0 → 41.0; processing & logistics 8.5 → 9.6; overheads 4.0 → 4.0; margin 1.5 → 0.3. _(release when: Candidate asks for revenue and cost per litre, or a margin breakdown.)_
- Processing & logistics breakdown (₹ per litre): FY23 → FY25: transport 3.0 → 3.1; chilling 1.5 → 1.55; plant processing 2.5 → 2.55; product loss & downgrading 1.5 → 2.4. _(release when: Candidate digs into processing & logistics costs.)_
- Downgrading definition and economics: Milk that arrives with high bacterial count cannot be sold as liquid milk and is converted to SMP/ghee or discarded. On average each downgraded litre loses about ₹18 of realisation vs liquid milk. The share of milk downgraded rose from ~4% to ~9% of intake. _(release when: Candidate asks what 'product loss & downgrading' is or why it rose.)_
- Power supply at collection centres: Since mid-2023, rural feeders in several districts get rationed three-phase power (4-6 hours of cuts a day). BMCs with diesel generator backup run them only sporadically because of diesel cost; around 900 BMCs have no backup at all. _(release when: Candidate asks why bacterial counts rose at particular BMCs, or asks about chilling/power/infrastructure.)_
- Solar-hybrid chiller option: A solar-hybrid retrofit costs about ₹6 lakh per BMC and keeps milk chilled through power cuts. Vendors can retrofit ~300 BMCs per quarter. _(release when: Candidate proposes fixing chilling/power at BMCs and asks about options or costs.)_
**Exhibit "Milk downgraded by collection route, FY25"** _(release when: Candidate asks where/which route the downgrading comes from, or asks for a breakdown of downgrading.)_ — unit: %
| Collection route | Share of intake volume | Share of milk downgraded |
|---|---|---|
| BMC with generator backup | 35% | 3% |
| BMC without backup | 45% | 16% |
| Direct to plant (<30 km) | 20% | 4% |
Note: FY23 downgrading was ~4% across all routes.
Hidden takeaways: Downgrading is concentrated in BMCs without power backup (16% vs 3-4% elsewhere). Those BMCs carry 45% of volume, so they explain almost all of the rise to ~9%.
**HIDDEN — never reveal during the case:**
- Key insights: Procurement price rises (+₹3/L) have been mostly passed through in realisation (+₹2.9/L); the controllable squeeze is in processing & logistics (+₹1.1/L). Almost all of that is product loss & downgrading (+₹0.9/L), about ₹33 cr a year. Downgrading is concentrated at BMCs without power backup after rural power rationing: an operational cold-chain problem, not a pricing problem.
- Calculations: What is the annual cost of the extra downgrading? → Annual intake = 10 lakh L/day × 365 ≈ 36.5 crore litres. Extra downgrading = 9% - 4% = 5% → ~1.8 crore litres × ₹18 ≈ ₹33 crore a year. Total margin drop = ₹1.2/L × 36.5 cr L ≈ ₹44 crore, so downgrading explains ~75% of it. | Payback of solar-hybrid retrofits for BMCs without backup? → 900 × ₹6 lakh = ₹54 crore. If downgrading at those BMCs falls from 16% to ~4%, saving ≈ 12% × 45% × 36.5 cr L × ₹18 ≈ ₹35 crore/yr. Payback ≈ 1.5 years.
- Reference solution: Break margin per litre into realisation, procurement, processing & logistics, and overheads. Realisation growth has roughly matched procurement growth, so the squeeze comes from processing & logistics, and within that from product loss & downgrading (₹1.5 → ₹2.4 per litre). Downgrading doubled because milk sits unchilled at BMCs without power backup during rationed power supply (16% downgraded vs 3-4%). That costs ~₹33 cr a year. Fix the cold chain: solar-hybrid retrofits (₹54 cr, ~1.5-year payback), diesel/genset operating support in the interim, collection-time windows, and a quality-linked incentive for societies.
- Other valid approaches: A value-chain/process approach that follows the milk from farm to consumer is equally valid and often finds the loss faster. Candidates may also propose product-mix optimisation (sell more curd/ghee at better margin). Valid as a secondary lever, but it doesn't address the root cause.
- Recommendation: Treat this as a cold-chain problem. (1) Immediately: fund diesel for generator backup at the worst BMCs and shorten collection-to-chilling time. (2) Over 3-4 quarters: retrofit the ~900 BMCs without backup with solar-hybrid chillers (~₹54 cr, ~1.5-year payback), prioritising by volume and downgrade rate. (3) Introduce a milk-quality (bacterial count) incentive for societies. This recovers ~₹30+ cr/yr, bringing margin back toward 2.5-3%. Pricing actions are a last resort given political constraints. Risks: vendor capacity, maintenance of solar units, and continued power-supply deterioration.
- Common pitfalls: Stopping at 'procurement costs went up' without checking pass-through. Recommending price changes that the constraints rule out. Missing that the exhibit concentrates the problem in one route.

**Opening (read this aloud to start):** Our client sells subscription point-of-sale and inventory software to restaurants in India. **Objective (share if asked):** Diagnose why revenue has stalled despite strong new sales, and say what to fix first.
**Background (share only the part asked about):** About 18,000 restaurant customers at the start of the year. Monthly subscription averages ₹2,000 per outlet. Sells to independent restaurants and small chains. Pricing unchanged for two years.
**Clarification answers (give only the one asked about):**
- pricing / revenue model: Flat monthly subscription, averaging ₹2,000 per outlet. No price changes in two years. There are no significant add-on products.
- customer segments: Mostly independent restaurants (about 80% of customers) plus small chains of 2-10 outlets.
**Data — release ONLY when its condition is met:**
- Customer flows, last 12 months: Customers at start of year: 18,000. New customers added: 7,200 (up from 5,000 the previous year). Customers lost: 7,300. End of year: ~17,900. _(release when: Candidate asks about number of customers, new customers vs churn, or retention.)_
- Churn reasons (exit interviews): Of churned customers: 45% closed down (restaurant shut); 35% switched to a competitor offering free POS bundled with an online-ordering aggregator; 20% cited poor onboarding/support. _(release when: Candidate asks why customers leave.)_
**Exhibit "Annual churn rate by customer tenure"** _(release when: Candidate asks which customers churn or asks to segment churn.)_ — unit: % of customers lost in the year
Churn rate: 0-6 months = 58; 6-12 months = 36; 1-2 years = 18; 2+ years = 9
Note: Tenure at the start of the period.
Hidden takeaways: Churn is concentrated in the first year, so it is an onboarding/fit problem and the new-customer engine is feeding a leaky bucket. Established customers are sticky (9%).
**HIDDEN — never reveal during the case:**
- Key insights: The problem is not acquisition: gross adds are up 44%, but ~40% annual churn wipes them out. Churn is concentrated in the first year of tenure, so onboarding and customer fit are the fixable parts; restaurant closures (45%) are structural.

- Other valid approaches: Cohort-based analysis of revenue retention, which reaches the same conclusion. Starting from sales-team incentives (paid on signed deals, so they bring in poor-fit customers) is a strong hypothesis-led route.
- Recommendation: Stop pouring customers into a leaky bucket: move sales incentives to 6-month retained customers, qualify out high-risk new restaurants, build a 90-day onboarding programme, and add integrations with aggregators to neutralise the free bundled POS. Target halving first-year churn, which would restore ~₹8-9 cr of annual run-rate. Risk: slower headline sign-ups in the short term.
- Common pitfalls: Suggesting price increases or more sales hiring without noticing churn. Reading the exhibit as 'churn is high' without the tenure implication.

## CASE seed-strategy-tea — strategy · Agriculture / Plantations (tea) · standard, ~25 min
**Opening (read this aloud to start):** Our client owns 12 tea estates in Assam and sells most of its tea through auctions. It has been loss-making for three years. The board is asking: should we sell some estates, invest to turn them around, or move into branded tea? What would you advise?
**Objective (share if asked):** Recommend a strategic direction that returns the company to profitability within three years.
**Background (share only the part asked about):** Produces ~18 million kg of made tea a year, mostly CTC. Estates are a mix of old bushes and newer plantings. Labour is ~60% of production cost and wages are set by state regulation.
**Clarification answers (give only the one asked about):**
- financials: Revenue ~₹400 cr, operating loss ~₹30 cr last year. The company has limited cash and moderate debt.
- market: Auction prices for CTC tea have been flat in nominal terms for five years while wages have risen ~8% a year.
- options: The board is open to any combination, including selling estates.
**Data — release ONLY when its condition is met:**
- Estate-level economics: Cost of production ranges from ₹190/kg at the best estates to ₹310/kg at the worst. Average auction realisation is ₹220/kg for most estates; three estates with better quality realise ₹280/kg. _(release when: Candidate asks about estate-level profitability or cost variation.)_
- Branded tea: Branded packaged tea earns ₹350-450/kg equivalent net of distribution but needs heavy marketing; national brands dominate. Regional brands succeed mostly in their home states. Building a brand would take ~₹60-80 cr over 3 years. _(release when: Candidate asks about branded tea or moving downstream.)_
- Estate sale market: Buyers exist for estates with good bushes and yields. Low-yield estates sell for little more than land value; some have been sold to smallholder-aggregator companies. _(release when: Candidate asks about selling estates or what they're worth.)_
**Exhibit "Estate portfolio: yield and cost of production"** _(release when: Candidate asks to segment the estates or compare their performance.)_

- Key insights: It isn't one company-wide answer: estate group C (old bushes, low yield) causes almost all the loss, and A is very profitable. Sell or restructure C (e.g. lease to smallholder aggregators, or replant over time), fix B's cost, and protect A. - Reference solution: Reframe the board's either/or: segment the estates. The exhibit shows A profitable, B break-even and C losing ~₹43 cr. Sell or restructure C (land value plus smallholder partnerships), run a cost and yield programme in B (mechanised plucking, infilling), and move A's high-quality output from auctions to private sales, exports and a small premium/online brand rather than a full national brand. That returns the company to profit within ~2 years.
- Other valid approaches: An option-evaluation matrix (sell / turnaround / brand) that ends with a mixed answer by estate. A 'replant C' option with a long payback (tea bushes take ~4-5 years to mature) can be defended if the candidate quantifies the cash need.
- Recommendation: Don't pick one option for the whole company. (1) Exit or restructure the 4 group-C estates (sale, or lease to smallholder aggregators): removes ~₹43 cr of losses. (2) Run a 2-year productivity programme in the 5 group-B estates. (3) Take group A's premium tea direct: private sales, exports and a small premium online brand, instead of betting ₹60-80 cr on a national brand. Result: profitable within ~2 years. Risks: labour relations and government approvals for estate sales, and auction price volatility.
- Common pitfalls: Picking one of the three options for the whole company. Jumping to branding because margins look higher.
